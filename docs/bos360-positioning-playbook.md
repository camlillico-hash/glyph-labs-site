# BOS360 positioning playbook

Private working notes for warm introductions, discovery calls and follow-ups. Diagnose the company before describing an engagement. Use the language that fits the conversation rather than reciting a pitch.

## Core positioning

**One sentence:** BOS360 helps founder-led leadership teams build the management system they need to run the company with greater clarity, accountability and consistency — without everything depending on the founder.

Natural variants:

- “I help growing founder-led companies put a stronger way of running the business around a leadership team that has outgrown the informal one.”
- “BOS360 connects direction, priorities, accountability and the leadership conversations needed to keep execution moving.”
- “My work is to help the team run the company together, so the founder does not have to hold the whole system together.”

Use “operating system” when it helps. If it sounds abstract, describe what changes in decisions, ownership and follow-through.

## Reasoning sequence

1. **Symptoms.** Start with what the prospect is experiencing in their own words: recurring issues, shifting priorities, unproductive meetings or founder bottlenecks.
2. **Underlying operating problem.** Ask how direction becomes priorities, how decisions get made, and what happens between planning sessions. Look for a management system that has not kept pace with growth.
3. **Consequences.** Understand what the problem costs in time, momentum, missed opportunities, team confidence or founder attention. Do not assume the cost.
4. **Desired state.** Ask what the leadership team should be able to do more reliably six or twelve months from now.
5. **BOS360 mechanism.** Explain only the pieces that speak to their diagnosis: shared direction, priority discipline, useful measures, a rhythm for solving issues, clear ownership and better leadership conversations.
6. **Engagement.** Discuss how working together could start once the actual problem and readiness are clear. Avoid leading with a generic cadence or planning-day specification.

**Example:** “Our quarterly planning isn’t very effective.” Do not immediately sell a better quarterly session. Ask what happens afterward. Do priorities survive daily demands? How are they measured? Who owns them? What happens when something slips? Where do unresolved issues go? How involved does the founder remain? A useful reflection might be: “This may not be mainly a quarterly-planning problem. It sounds like the management rhythm between sessions is not giving the team enough clarity or accountability.”

## Incorrect mental models

| Prospect thinks Cam is… | Acknowledge the overlap | Clarify the distinction | Natural response |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A meeting facilitator | Good facilitation is part of the work. | The purpose is a stronger system for decisions and follow-through, not a better meeting in isolation. | “I do facilitate the room, but I’m looking at what happens before and after it too. Are the right issues reaching the team, and do decisions turn into action?” |
| An EOS implementer with different terminology | Both bring discipline to how a company runs. | BOS360 uses its own model and adapts to what the company already does well. | “There is some common ground in getting a team aligned and accountable. I’m less interested in replacing a working tool than in making the whole system work for your company.” |
| A strategy consultant | Strategic clarity matters. | Cam works with the team to connect direction to choices and execution; he does not return with an answer deck. | “We may need to get sharper on strategy, but I’m not coming in to write one for you. The team needs to make the choices and know how to carry them through.” |
| An executive coach | Individual leaders grow through the work. | The unit of change is the leadership team and how the company operates. | “I coach leaders in the course of the work, but the aim is better collective decisions and execution.” |
| A fractional COO | Operational discipline is involved. | Cam does not own a function or execute departmental work. | “I won’t take the COO seat. I help your team create the clarity and accountability that make it easier for them to run the business.” |
| Someone hired to create OKRs | Measurable priorities may be useful. | A goal format alone does not solve ownership, issue resolution or leadership behaviour. | “If OKRs are working, we can keep them. The question is whether they are changing what the team does week to week.” |
| Someone hired only for annual or quarterly planning | Planning creates valuable direction. | The management rhythm between sessions determines whether plans turn into results. | “The planning day matters, but I care just as much about what happens the next month, when real trade-offs show up.” |

## Aha moments to listen for

- “We have a direction, but we do not have a reliable way to execute it.”
- “Our meetings reflect the problem; they are not the whole problem.”
- “I am still the glue holding too much of this together.”
- “We have good people, but their knowledge is not turning into coordinated decisions.”
- “We keep solving the same issues because we never settle the underlying one.”
- “We set priorities, then let urgent work quietly replace them.”
- “The team agrees in the room, but ownership is unclear afterward.”
- “We have numbers, but they do not help us decide what to do.”
- “We avoid difficult conversations until they become expensive.”
- “What worked with ten people does not work with fifty.”
- “The team needs a way to run the company that does not depend on me remembering everything.”

## Discovery questions

Choose a few. Follow the answer rather than running through the list.

**Founder dependency**

- Which decisions still come back to you that you expected the team to own by now?
- What stops moving when you are away for two weeks?

**Direction and priorities**

- If I asked each leader for the company’s three most important priorities, how similar would their answers be?
- What did the team decide to stop doing when it chose this quarter’s priorities?
- Which priority is most likely to be displaced by urgent work, and what protects it?

**Accountability and execution**

- When a commitment slips, how does the team find out and what happens next?
- Which important initiative has been nearly finished for too long?
- Where is ownership shared in a way that makes it hard to know who will act?

**Measurement**

- Which numbers help your leaders make decisions before a result is already missed?
- Where are you relying on confidence or anecdotes because progress is hard to see?

**Meetings and unresolved issues**

- What decisions did your last leadership meeting produce?
- Which issue has appeared in three meetings without being resolved?
- What happens to an issue that is important but does not fit the week’s agenda?

**Leadership team and complexity**

- What is the conversation the team knows it needs to have but keeps postponing?
- Where do functional goals pull leaders in different directions?
- What changed as the company grew that your way of managing it has not yet caught up with?
- If the team were running the company noticeably better a year from now, what would you see happening without your intervention?

## Close the loop

Reflect the diagnosis in the prospect’s language before explaining BOS360. Ask whether it feels accurate. Then describe the smallest set of mechanisms that addresses it. The desired insight is that the company needs a better way to run itself, not another isolated tool or planning session.
